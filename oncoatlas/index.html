Part one · How a cancer begins

Cells, genes and the controls that keep tissues in balance, and what happens when those controls fail. No background needed.

i

Chapter oneScale · 10⁻⁵ m

Thirty trillion cells, one set of instructions.

Nearly every cell in your body carries the same DNA: about two metres of it, folded into a nucleus a hundredth of a millimetre across. Cells read different parts of it to become skin, blood or bone. Cancer starts when some of those instructions change and a cell stops following the rules that keep a tissue in balance.

ii

Chapter twoScale · genes → clones

A stuck accelerator and cut brakes.

Genes that control growth come in two broad kinds. Oncogenes work like an accelerator: one faulty copy can jam it down. Tumour suppressor genes work like brakes: usually both copies have to fail. Most cancers need several such hits, collected over years, in one line of cells.

Two copies of every gene

Hit a copy and see what changes.

You inherit one copy of each gene from each parent. Click a copy to mutate it.

Selection inside a tissue

Grow a clone.

Each square is a cell in the lining of an organ, sitting on its basement membrane. Normal cells divide only to replace cells that die. When a cell picks up a hit, every daughter inherits it. A cell with the right combination outgrows its neighbours, and its descendants form a clone. Choose a hit and click a cell, or let mutations arise at random.

A teaching model. Real tissues hold billions of cells and take years; mutation rates here are accelerated enormously so selection is visible in a minute. Colours mark how many hits a cell carries.

What a pathologist might call it

Normal

Hit to add when you click a cell

    Drivers and passengers. A typical tumour carries thousands of mutations. Most are passengers that change nothing. A small number, often four or five in a common adult cancer, are drivers that give the clone its edge. Peter Nowell described cancer as this kind of step-by-step evolution in 1976.

    Because the clone keeps evolving, cells in different parts of one tumour can differ. That is one reason a treatment can shrink most of a tumour and still leave a resistant group behind.

    Why inherited cancers appear earlier. Someone born with one faulty copy of a brake gene, such as BRCA1 or RB1, already carries the first hit in every cell. One more chance hit can be enough, so their cancers tend to start younger and sometimes on both sides of the body. Alfred Knudson's 1971 study of the eye cancer retinoblastoma first set out this two-hit idea.
    iii

    Chapter threeScale · cell behaviour

    The hallmarks: what a cell must gain to become a cancer.

    In 2000 Douglas Hanahan and Robert Weinberg proposed that most, perhaps all, cancers acquire a shared set of capabilities. They updated the framework in 2011, and Hanahan extended it in 2022. It also maps cleanly onto treatment: many modern drugs aim at a single hallmark.

    iv

    Chapter fourScale · tissue over years

    Becoming cancer, one boundary at a time.

    Carcinomas, the most common cancers, begin in an epithelium, the cell sheet that lines a surface. What separates a precursor from a cancer, and a cancer from a metastasis, is usually a boundary that has been crossed.

    The metastatic cascade

    Six steps, each a filter that most cells fail.

    Large numbers of tumour cells can enter the blood, yet only a tiny fraction found a metastasis. Each step selects for cells that can survive the next environment. In 1889 Stephen Paget called this the seed and soil problem: where a cancer spreads depends on both the cell and the organ it reaches.

      v

      Chapter fiveScale · tissue of origin

      A cancer is named for the cell it came from.

      "Cancer" covers more than a hundred diseases. The first split is whether a growth is benign or malignant. The second is the kind of cell it started in, which sets its name, how it behaves and how it is treated.

      Benign or malignant

      Both are tumours. Only one invades.

      "Tumour" means a swelling or mass, benign or malignant. Hippocrates called malignant tumours karkinos, Greek for crab, probably because the finger-like projections spreading from a tumour called a crab to mind. The Roman writer Celsus translated it into Latin as cancer. The physician Galen later used oncos, Greek for swelling, which gives us oncology.

      Families of cancer

      Ten families by tissue of origin.

      Reading the name

      Tissue + ending = diagnosis.

      Most tumour names join a word for the tissue to an ending. -oma on its own usually means benign. -carcinoma means malignant and from a lining (epithelium). -sarcoma means malignant and from connective tissue. Pick a tissue and a behaviour.

      Exceptions. A few "-oma" names are malignant by convention: melanoma, lymphoma, seminoma, mesothelioma and myeloma. Glioma covers a range from slow-growing to aggressive. When in doubt, the report will say "malignant" or "benign".

      vi

      Chapter sixScale · a lifetime

      Where the hits come from, and which ones we can avoid.

      Every cancer needs mutations. Some come from the unavoidable wear of copying DNA over a lifetime. Others are caused by tobacco, sunlight, alcohol, infections or body weight, and those can be reduced. A few are inherited.

      Five sources of damage

      Each leaves its own fingerprint in the DNA.

      Different causes change DNA in different ways. Sequencing a tumour can reveal these patterns, called mutational signatures, years after the exposure.

        Age

        Most cancers are found in later life.

        Hits pile up with every year and every division, and the body's defences weaken with age. Rates climb from fewer than 26 new cancers per 100,000 people a year under age 20 to more than 1,000 per 100,000 from age 60. Half of all US cancers are diagnosed at 67 or older. Childhood cancers are rare and mostly different diseases, usually arising from developing tissue rather than from decades of damage.

        Four in ten cancers in US adults are linked to risks that can be changed.

        Chapter sevenScale · 10⁻⁵ → 10⁻¹ m

        From one cell to a lump you can feel.

        A tumour that doubles in size again and again grows invisibly for most of its life. That hidden stretch is why screening exists: to find some cancers, or their precursors, before they cause symptoms.

        Drag to double

        Doublings30
        Cells
        Diameter · weight

        An idealised curve. Real tumours do not double on a timetable. Many cells die along the way, growth usually slows as a tumour gets bigger, and doubling times range from weeks to years. The arithmetic still holds: a 1 cm tumour has already passed about 30 of the 40 doublings that would take it to a kilogram. The usual rule of thumb is a billion cells per gram; because tumours also contain blood vessels and supporting tissue, the true number of cancer cells may be nearer a hundred million.

        Screening

        Tests for people without symptoms.

        These are the US Preventive Services Task Force recommendations for people at average risk. Other countries choose different ages and intervals, and people at higher risk often start earlier.

        Symptoms worth checking

        Most have harmless causes. Persistent ones deserve a look.

          In plain wordsA symptom that is new, unexplained and lasts more than a few weeks is a reason to see a doctor. It is rarely cancer. When it is, finding it earlier usually means more treatment options.

          Deaths per case is the mortality-to-incidence ratio. It is a crude, population-level signal: a ratio near 1 means that, across the world, almost as many people die of the cancer each year as are diagnosed with it. Pancreatic, liver and oesophageal cancers sit near the top because they are often found late and are hard to treat. Thyroid and prostate cancers sit near the bottom because many are slow-growing and many are detected early.

          Not a survival rate. The ratio compares two counts from the same year, which come from different people. It also hides large differences between countries. For a person's outlook, stage-specific survival (Chapter ix) is far more informative.

          Chapter nineScale · 10⁻¹ m

          Five organs, five ways to measure extent.

          The T in TNM is measured differently in each organ. In the bowel and bladder it is depth through the wall. In the lung and breast it is mostly size. In the prostate it is whether the tumour has broken out of the gland. Move the tumour and watch its category change.

          The grammar of a stage.

          A string like ypT2 N1a M0 packs in when the assessment was made, what evidence it rests on, and how far the cancer reached. Type or paste one to see it taken apart.

          Try
          cClinical. Examination, imaging and biopsy before treatment.
          pPathological. Adds the resection specimen when its requirements are met.
          yAssessed after initial systemic or radiation therapy.
          rRecurrence, staged after a disease-free interval.
          aDetermined at autopsy.

          No stage group is calculated here. Turning T, N and M into a stage (I to IV) needs the organ's current AJCC tables, and for some organs also grade, biomarkers or PSA. The organ notes summarise AJCC 8th-edition concepts, plus Version 9 for lung, in plain words.

          Chapter elevenScale · 10⁰ → 10⁻¹⁰ m

          Every instrument sees one slice of scale.

          A PET scan can survey the whole body but cannot resolve anything smaller than a few millimetres. A microscope resolves details of a fraction of a micrometre but sees only a sliver of tissue. Diagnosis means stitching their views together.

          xii

          Chapter twelveScale · 10⁻⁹ m

          A biomarker is a measurement. First ask what was measured.

          Genes, RNA and proteins sit at different depths in the cell, and each is read by a different test. Many errors in reading reports come from treating a protein result and a gene result as the same thing.

          Result states

          Six words that are easy to misread.

          Detected

          The target was found and reported under the assay's rules. Whether it matters still depends on the exact variant and the cancer.

          Not detected

          A valid test did not find the target within what it covers and how sensitive it is. This is a bounded negative.

          Pending

          The result is not final. It is not negative, and decisions may wait for it.

          Indeterminate

          The signal cannot be classified with confidence. The report may suggest a different test.

          Insufficient

          The sample did not meet the test's requirements. This is a technical failure, not a biological answer.

          Not performed

          No test was done. No conclusion either way.

          xiii

          Chapter thirteenScale · one site → whole body

          Treatments differ in where they reach and when they are given.

          Surgery and radiation treat a place. Drugs treat the body. Further down, see how each treatment actually kills a cancer cell. Modern therapies blur the line by carrying a payload to wherever a target is found. The same drug can be curative, preventive or palliative depending on when it is used.

          Mechanisms

          How each treatment kills a cancer cell.

          Pick a treatment, then switch it on. Each scene is a simplified diagram of the mechanism, not a picture of real cells.

          Timing words

          Neoadjuvant, adjuvant, definitive. The word depends on what comes first.

          Intent is a separate question. Curative intent means the plan aims for cure; it is not a promise. Palliative cancer treatment aims to control disease or symptoms when cure is not the expected outcome. Supportive palliative care can start at diagnosis and run alongside curative treatment.

          Clinical trials

          How a new treatment earns its place.

          Every standard treatment on this page was once experimental. New treatments are tested in phases. Each phase asks a different question and usually involves more people than the one before.

          Placebos are rare in cancer trials. When an effective treatment exists, people in the comparison group get the current standard treatment, not a dummy pill. A placebo is used only alongside standard care or when no proven treatment exists.

          What this is

          A visual course in cancer for people without a medical background: from cells and genes to causes, screening, diagnosis, treatment and the documents patients receive. All cases and reports are invented.

          What this is not

          Not diagnostic, not a staging calculator, and not treatment advice. Thresholds, editions and drug indications change; check current organ-specific guidance and talk to the care team.
